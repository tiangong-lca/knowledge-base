Table 1. Positioning and applicability boundaries of five future-oriented and temporally resolved life cycle assessment (LCA) approaches across temporal resolution, technological maturity, background and impact-assessment treatment, and primary limitations.

| Approach and key literature | Temporal positioning and resolution | Technological maturity and scale-up focus | Background system and impact-assessment treatment | Primary applicability boundaries and limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prospective LCA (recommended umbrella term; Arvidsson et al. 2024; Steubing and de Koning 2021) | Targets a specific future year or period, typically solved as a point-in-time aggregate | Assesses emerging technologies as well as mature technologies under future background conditions | Couples integrated assessment models (IAMs) into future background databases (e.g., superstructure + scenario difference file) to avoid foreground–background temporal mismatch | Superstructure architecture resolves software-engineering bottlenecks only and does not guarantee scenario quality; ghost processes, uncertainty propagation, and licensing limits persist |
| Ex-ante LCA (Arvidsson et al. 2024; Tsoy et al. 2020) | Targets a future point prior to commercialization and large-scale deployment | Necessarily models upscaling from laboratory or pilot scale to industrial maturity | Estimates industrial inventories via the three-step upscaling framework using process simulation, manual calculations, molecular structure models, or proxy data, then couples with future backgrounds | Across 18 reviewed cases, 0 reported technology readiness levels (TRLs) and 0 completed post-commercialization empirical validation; expert involvement and flowchart disclosure were widely lacking |
| Anticipatory LCA (Arvidsson et al. 2024) | Targets future development pathways of emerging technologies | Shares ex-ante LCA's focus on early-stage technology scale-up | Beyond foreground and background modeling, emphasizes stakeholder participation in selecting system boundaries, functional units, impact categories, and weights | Results depend heavily on stakeholder value preferences and governance framing; cannot substitute for empirical technological measurements |
| Laboratory-scale LCA (Arvidsson et al. 2024; Tsoy et al. 2020) | Current or near-term laboratory research stage | Retains low maturity without industrial scale-up | Uses direct laboratory measurements or proxy data, typically paired with current background databases | Suited only to early research-and-development hotspot diagnosis; cannot be compared directly against mature commercial industrial technologies |
| Dynamic LCA (Lueddeckens et al. 2020; Arvidsson et al. 2024) | Resolves emission timing and impact response across time steps (distinguishing assessment, inventory, and characterization time horizons) | Not restricted to emerging technologies; focuses on tracking emission timing across the life cycle | Timestamped inventory alone is semi-dynamic; coupling dynamic inventory with dynamic characterization factors constitutes fully dynamic LCA | Most impact categories lack dynamic characterization factors, and supply-chain scheduling data and software remain limited; time horizons, discounting, and dynamic normalization embed normative value choices |

Once spatial and temporal scales are fixed, aggregating multiple environmental categories onto a single scale raises a further valuation question: [Monetary Valuation of Environmental Damage](/en/docs/book/lime-preferences-and-damage-weights/) next examines stated-preference weighting of environmental damages. For the underlying worked examples on emission timing and long-run capacity-expansion grids, see [Climate Equivalence](/en/docs/book/climate-equivalence/) and [Electricity Systems](/en/docs/book/electricity-service-and-response/).
