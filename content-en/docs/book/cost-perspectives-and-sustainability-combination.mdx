Table 1. Comparison of accounting actors, discounting conventions, and integration boundaries across five life cycle costing and monetary valuation paradigms.

| Monetary accounting or valuation paradigm | Accounting actor and decision question | Internal transactions and taxes | Discounting convention | Unpriced environmental / social consequences | Boundary for integration into LCSA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional LCC (from the 1933 US Government Accountability Office (GAO) tradition) | Single organization (typically buyer total cost of ownership [TCO] or manufacturer internal cost) | Counts only that actor's own cash inflows and outflows; often omits upstream external stages or end-of-life | Discounted to net present value (NPV) at the firm's opportunity cost of capital | Off-ledger externalities are excluded | System boundary is often narrower than the physical life cycle; functional units must be aligned before pairing |
| Environmental LCC (Swarr et al. 2011 / Heijungs et al. 2013) | Real monetary flows across one or more supply-chain actors under an equivalent functional unit | Intermediate commodity prices cancel (− and +) in matrix value added; includes current and near-term expected internalized taxes or subsidies (e.g., within 2 years) | Selects private or social discount rate to fit the decision goal, paired with a 0% undiscounted sensitivity baseline | Strictly prohibits converting uninternalized environmental damages into currency and adding them | Complements environmental LCA (E-LCA) and social LCA (S-LCA) without double-counting the same emissions |
| Societal LCC (UNEP/SETAC 2011 Fig. 5) | Society-wide welfare perspective combining all private costs/benefits with monetized externalities | Cancels pure fiscal transfers among actors (already paid environmental taxes cannot be added again to full shadow damages) | Typically applies a lower social discount rate for intertemporal public welfare | Incorporates climate, pollution, and other external damages via shadow prices or abatement costs | Where environmental damages are already monetized, they must not be summed again with E-LCA physical indicators |
| Stated-preference environmental damage valuation (LIME2 / ISO 14008; see [Monetary Valuation of Environmental Damage](/en/docs/book/lime-preferences-and-damage-weights/)) | Surveyed public's stated or revealed willingness to pay (WTP) to avoid marginal health or ecosystem damage | Non-market preference weights (e.g., LIME2 assigns 1.47 × 10⁷ JPY/DALY); does not represent corporate cash expenditure | Depends on endpoint exposure integration horizon and survey scenario design | Covers selected use and non-use values, bounded by income effects and unmodeled pathways | Functions as a life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) weighting factor; if combined into full cost (e.g., 3.74% in the Japanese washing machine case), guard against double counting |
| Resource future extra extraction cost endpoint (ReCiPe 2016 USD2013; see [Resources, Land, and Biodiversity](/en/docs/book/resources-land-and-biodiversity/)) | Extra extraction cost imposed on future humanity as depletion forces a shift to lower-grade ores or marginal oil/gas fields | Macro geological grade-production regression integral (e.g., crude oil 0.457 USD2013/kg); excludes current market purchase price | ReCiPe 2016 fossil endpoint computes undiscounted mean future cumulative extra cost | Represents abiotic resource scarcity only; excludes pollution health impacts and labor conditions | Environmental endpoint indicator rather than financial expenditure; never add directly to buyer LCC cash flows |

Table 2. Applicability boundaries across integrated sustainability displays, multi-criteria decision analysis, and neighboring assessment tools.

| Integrated display or neighboring paradigm | Zero / reference definition | Cross-dimensional compensation and veto mechanism | Behavior on the same workwear case | Non-physical factor triggering conclusion reversal | Native scope and complementary role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Side-by-side profile with missing data flagged (Finkbeiner 2010 / UNEP/SETAC 2011 baseline) | Each pillar retains its native physical equivalents, risk hours, and monetary zero; missing data flagged separately | No cross-pillar summation; displays real trade-offs among environment, cost, and labor conditions explicitly | Shows directly that A lowers undiscounted spend (132.00 € vs. 140.00 € at 0%), C is low-carbon/high-price, and E carries forced-labor risk | No arithmetic masking; prioritizes verifying critical missing items capable of flipping the choice | Default baseline for early and routine LCSA to prevent single scores from flattening incommensurable goals |
| Intra-set min-max relative dashboard (LCSD, 0–1,000 seven-color scale) | Best option in candidate set = 1,000 (dark green), worst = 0 (dark red); missing data marked violet | Arithmetic or weighted average within and across themes; by default allows high scores to compensate 0-score items | Among A, B, and C, A (722.22) beats B (583.33); adding strictly dominated D flips B (816.67) ahead of A (800.00) | Violates independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA); a dominated decoy alters min-max denominators | Shows relative rank within the current candidate set only; 0 is neither zero impact nor an absolute red line |
| Compensatory utility and triangle (LCST / MAUT / weighted linear sum) | Internal or external normalization to dimensionless utility; three pillar weights sum to 100% | Full compensation: weakness on any dimension can be offset by sufficiently high scores on others | Under equal-weight min-max scoring of A, B, and E, forced-labor Bid E wins with 666.67 via top carbon and cost scores | Weight-vector selection (equal weighting is still a subjective value choice) and utility-function curvature | Suitable when no hard compliance floor is breached and decision-makers explicitly accept marginal substitution rates |
| Non-compensatory outranking with veto thresholds (ELECTRE / PROMETHEE; GLAM Phase 3) | Pairwise difference comparison with indifference, strict preference, and insurmountable veto thresholds | Non-compensatory or partially compensatory: exceeding a veto threshold on one criterion blocks outranking outright | Although E holds a 2/3 concordance majority on carbon and cost, its 60 h social-risk gap triggers the 50 h veto line and blocks outranking | Calibration of veto thresholds, sorting profiles, and concordance cut-offs | Enforces hard constraints—such as bans on child or forced labor and statutory limits—that low prices cannot offset |
| Eco-efficiency and site / regional neighboring tools (Eco-efficiency / RA / EIA / MFA-IOA) | Eco-efficiency divides by cost or value added; RA tests PEC/PNEC against 1 | Eco-efficiency tracks an environmental-to-monetary ratio; RA and EIA enforce independent site-level legal concentration limits | Cradle-to-gate positive net value added produces negative LCC; 10 g concentrated discharge breaches local limits while 20 g dispersed across 10 rivers stays compliant | Ratios hide totals and diverge near zero cost; local hydrology and sector aggregation shift conclusions | Site permitting belongs to RA/EIA; national and regional stock sources and sinks belong to MFA/SFA/IOA |
| Planetary-boundary absolute budget ratio (AESA; GLAM Table 3 / strong-sustainability doughnut) | Ratio of impact to allocated safe operating space ≤ 1 indicates compliance | Strong sustainability forbids offsetting ecological overshoot across boundaries or with economic gains | For A' (20 kg, 240 €) vs. B' (16 kg, 120 €), expenditure-share allocation passes A' (0.833) and fails B' (1.333); an equal 18.0 kg service allowance completely reverses the verdict | Ethical sharing principle used to downscale global budgets to products (expenditure share vs. equal service) | Evaluates macro or sectoral absolute ecological ceilings; currently lacks human health and abiotic resource boundaries |
