title: Reading Historical Sources
description: Compare early container and policy studies, framework workshops, handbooks, and software release notes to distinguish what each record documents and what historical connections it can support.
---

Reading a historical record in life cycle assessment (LCA) starts with identifying the exact event it documents. A report's title page, a workshop project page, a guidance manual, and a software handbook all carry dates, yet those dates rarely refer to the same kind of event. The sections below use container reports, assessment frameworks, and software records to show how to verify the evidentiary basis of historical claims.

## Reports and Publication Dates [#reports-and-publication-dates]

<Prose ids="cl-early-policy-question-version cl-early-container-commission" />

When comparing two container reports, begin by writing down the distinct question each study asked: one accounted for the activities required to deliver a shared beverage-supply task, whereas the other evaluated the rationale for national policy intervention. Next, separate the title-page date from reprint notices on the physical or digitized copy so that two printings of the same study are not mistaken for two separate studies. When identical text is reprinted later, the reprint date records when that copy was issued; it does not move the original study forward in time. Faced with similarly titled reports, compare authors, commissioning bodies, and main text to confirm whether they are the same work. For the underlying materials, see [Beverage Container Studies](/en/docs/book/early-container-comparisons/).

## Workshops and Framework Activities [#workshops-and-framework-activities]

<Prose ids="cl-book-framework-project cl-book-framework-epa1993 cl-book-framework-unep" />

A project page's workshop dates, draft dates, and report publication dates each refer to a different milestone. Identifying the event, the document carrier, and the exact locus of a statement prevents substituting a later draft date for the date of a workshop. Locations, dates, and titles should also be cross-checked to determine whether two sources describe the same meeting. When comparing frameworks, establish what each numbered list counts: high-level phase divisions, framework components, and operational steps can all appear within a single handbook. Comparing the actual tasks assigned to each label—rather than counting labels alone—reveals whether two divisions differ in substance. For the primary records, see [Assessment Frameworks and Analytical Steps](/en/docs/book/framework-responsibilities/).

## Guidance Manuals and Actual Adoption [#guidance-manuals-and-actual-adoption]

<Prose ids="cl-book-framework-guide cl-book-framework-epa2006" />

When citing a guidance manual, specify the volume or section and distinguish rules, reference data, explanatory commentary, and retrospective summaries of earlier practice. If an executive summary and the main text give different names to the fourth phase, cite each location explicitly and compare the tasks assigned there rather than picking one name and claiming the entire work is uniform. Determining whether a later study actually adopted a manual requires checking which rules, models, or datasets that study implemented. That a manual was published and cited by a later study does not by itself establish what was adopted; operational records are required. See [Life Cycle Perspective (Cleaner Production and Preventive Use)](/en/docs/book/integrative-ambitions/#cleaner-production-and-preventive-use) and [Regionalization of Impact Assessment](/en/docs/book/localization-evidence-and-denominators/) for worked examples.

When auditing legacy models, record three items separately: version labels, exchange representations, and actual solver behavior. ## Chronology Versus Causation [#chronology-versus-causation]

Claiming that a method was "the first" requires first specifying exactly what event is meant. Locating an early surviving document supports reporting its date and the scope of the search conducted; when an author claims priority, attribute that claim to the author. Demonstrating first public presentation requires documenting which earlier candidate records were examined and excluded. Chronological sequence alone suggests only a possible connection. Showing that later work actually inherited or adopted an earlier method requires evidence in citations, archival records, or documented modeling practice. Keeping these evidentiary layers distinct ensures that when new records surface, it is clear which judgment must be revised.
